# Setouchi Triennale 2028 — proposal and options

Recommend **E1 Shared Sea Atlas** for a host-agreed indoor room on Awashima in autumn 2028. The paired world/Japan maps make the global material legible, while a paper table gives local collaborators a role in choosing the questions. E1 is a proposal, not a venue commitment.

The application draft is eight A4 pages; add verified applicant CV/prior work within the ten-page limit. The official deadline checked 2026-10-29 is 2026-11-02 10:00 JST.

## E1 — Shared Sea Atlas

A wall of paired maps connects the Seto Inland Sea to the larger systems that pass through it. Each world map has a Japan companion. Visitors move between the two scales, then leave a question on a paper table.

Awashima’s shipping history gives the work a local starting point. The Maritime Memorial Hall or another agreed indoor room is a candidate, not a booked venue. The work asks residents which routes and materials deserve attention.

One indoor room, approximately 6 × 8 m; at least 8 m of usable wall; six paired panels and one 1.8 × 0.8 m table.

Twelve pigment prints, each 1,117.6 × 483.9 mm; reusable hanging rails; printed Japanese/English labels; paper and pencils. No network connection is needed.

Visitor sequence:

1. Start with Earth and Water. Find Japan without placing it at the centre of the world.
2. Compare six world/Japan pairs: land, water, cables, atmosphere, refinery throughput and temperature.
3. At a seated table, add an anonymous question or draw a route that matters to island life. Visitors may take their contribution home.

Planning total: JPY 2,050,000. These are allowances, not quotations.

Human review: Confirm the room, wall loads, clear routes and host capacity. Ask island partners to select the local questions and agree how contributions will be kept or returned.

## E2 — The Port and the Cloud

Four map pairs show the physical systems behind a familiar word: cloud. Cables, data centres, ground stations and atmospheric clouds sit beside one another. A reading table makes their different meanings clear.

A port already connects visible movement with distant infrastructure. The display uses that experience without suggesting that a cable line proves a local landing or that a data-centre point measures electricity use.

Indoor room around 5 × 6 m; four paired wall panels; one low reading table; seated alternative to every wall text.

Eight full-size prints; freestanding reusable panels; a source booklet; paper question cards. Optional recorded conversation only after speaker agreement.

Visitor sequence:

1. Follow one cable route across the world plate.
2. Compare it with the groundstation and cloud/CDN inventories, keeping their dates visible.
3. Read the atmosphere plate as weather observations with distinct acquisition dates. Write one question about what remains unseen.

Planning total: JPY 1,700,000. These are allowances, not quotations.

Human review: Confirm a host room outside ferry circulation. Review infrastructure captions for unsupported claims. Obtain speaker and recording agreements before producing audio.

## E3 — Heat, Salt, Water

A small atlas puts land temperature, water, food, fisheries and oil refining beside one another. The installation invites comparison of systems while making their different units and time periods visible.

Utazu’s salt-making history offers a concrete entry into energy, evaporation and labour. Residents can correct the exhibition’s questions before the final labels are printed.

Indoor room around 6 × 6 m; five paired panels; one reading table; no process equipment or water installation.

Ten full-size prints; modular rails; source cards; tactile sample of folded paper. No extraction, salt production demonstration or environmental sensing is proposed.

Visitor sequence:

1. Begin with the water plate and a short account of the host’s chosen local history.
2. Read the temperature map as record-day counts, not as a forecast of island weather.
3. Compare food and fisheries statistics with refinery throughput. A caption explains what each measure can and cannot say about the islands.

Planning total: JPY 1,950,000. These are allowances, not quotations.

Human review: Agree the local historical account and credit contributors. Have a reader check that national statistics are not presented as measurements of Utazu.

## V1 — Fold a World, Read an Island

Participants cut, fold and annotate a paper world map. A world/Japan comparison becomes a practical lesson in how a map changes attention. Each person chooses whether to keep or share their sheet.

The supplied conference ideas include a rip, burn, fold workshop. This proposal develops the paper-folding part into a low-equipment public session; it uses no burning.

Two 90-minute sessions, up to 20 people each; a 6 × 6 m accessible room; five worktables and a seated demonstration.

A3 study prints, blunt scissors, pencils, reusable templates and large-print instructions. Full-size masters remain available as wall references.

Visitor sequence:

1. A ten-minute demonstration compares globe, flat map and Japan detail.
2. In pairs, fold an A3 study sheet and mark a route, material or question.
3. A closing table conversation lets participants explain their choices; no personal location data is collected.

Planning total: JPY 450,000. These are allowances, not quotations.

Human review: Confirm the host, participant age range and access needs. Review translations and provide a no-cut alternative. Photography requires separate participant agreement.

## V2 — What Does the Cloud Touch?

A moderated conversation connects digital networks to cables, ports, energy and everyday work. Three map pairs anchor the discussion. The audience leaves with a short source guide.

The conference memo names cloud/network and media themes. Nicole Starosielski, Jennifer Holt, Lisa Nakamura and Eric Rodenbeck are possible invitation leads, not confirmed contributors.

One 90-minute conversation for about 50 visitors; accessible indoor seating; three paired maps and a source table.

Six reference prints, microphones if the room requires them, bilingual handout and live interpretation allowance. No recording is assumed.

Visitor sequence:

1. A short map reading establishes what the sources measure.
2. Two invited contributors and a local respondent discuss one concrete route or material dependency.
3. Audience questions are followed by a facilitated source-table conversation, with an option to contribute in writing.

Planning total: JPY 780,000. These are allowances, not quotations.

Human review: Secure contributors, define a topic with the local respondent, and confirm interpretation. International travel beyond the allowance would require a revised budget or remote participation.

## V3 — An Island Seen from Two Scales

A short guided walk alternates between looking at the island and reading maps. Participants record observations in words or sketches. The activity ends at a seated table so walking is optional.

Megijima’s shore, wind walls and settlement offer places to discuss what a global map leaves out. A resident host selects the route and the subjects; this is not a survey of residents.

Two sessions of up to 12 people; route under 1 km if approved; an indoor alternative for weather or access.

A4 study sheets, clipboards, pencils and a large seated map. No tracking app, drone or collection of identifiable location histories.

Visitor sequence:

1. Meet indoors and choose a walking or seated version.
2. Visit three agreed stopping places over 45 minutes. Compare observed surroundings with dated map layers.
3. Return for a 30-minute table discussion. Keep sketches with their makers unless they explicitly choose to contribute them.

Planning total: JPY 350,000. These are allowances, not quotations.

Human review: Walk the route with the host; confirm step-free alternatives, tide/weather decisions and a cancellation contact. Keep visitor routes clear of working areas.

## I1 — The Port Reading Table

A durable table holds a sequence of world and Japan map sheets. A traveller can read one sheet in a minute or stay with the whole atlas. The table operates without a screen or network.

The format meets the variable time of a port visit. It must sit in a host-controlled room with free circulation, not in a boarding queue or an emergency route.

About 3 × 3 m including circulation; one 1.8 × 0.8 m table with seated reach and knee clearance; eight A2 study sheets.

Reusable timber or metal table, replaceable protected paper sheets, tabbed bindings and printed guide. Full print masters supply the source artwork.

Visitor sequence:

1. Choose a tab: water, cables, energy or heat.
2. Turn a world sheet to its Japan companion.
3. Read the date and one clear limitation; take a small printed guide if wanted.

Planning total: JPY 1,200,000. These are allowances, not quotations.

Human review: Confirm dimensions with access users and host staff; approve cleaning, sheet replacement and storage. Test glare and reading distance on a physical sample.

## I2 — Maps in the Wind’s Shelter

Three reversible fabric panels place world maps on one face and Japan details on the other. Visitors turn a panel by hand within a sheltered structure. The work makes scale a physical choice.

Ogijima’s dense settlement and wind-protection practices inform the idea. The structure must be designed for the actual site and must not attach to historic walls.

Indicative 4 × 4 m footprint; three 1.2 × 0.65 m panels; maximum proposed height 1.8 m. Dimensions are design assumptions pending site and engineering review.

Printed fabric, rounded reusable frames, controlled hinges and an engineered support. No ground anchoring or historic-wall fixing is assumed.

Visitor sequence:

1. Approach three panels at walking speed.
2. Turn one panel to compare world and Japan views.
3. Read a short seated copy of each label and return the panel to its resting position.

Planning total: JPY 2,350,000. These are allowances, not quotations.

Human review: Required before fabrication: site permission, structural review, wind and weather operating limits, pinch-point review and an indoor fallback. This is the most complex option.

## I3 — The Island Atlas Desk

An ordinary desk becomes a compact atlas of sea, food, energy and sky. Fold-out sheets and a source book let visitors read at their own pace. A host can change the sequence during the season.

The supplied site report connects Ibukijima with iriko production and a former school. A desk respects the scale of a room and leaves room for a locally chosen account of work and sea.

About 3 × 3 m; one adapted 1.6 × 0.8 m desk, two chairs and a wall reference. No power or internet is needed.

Repairable desk, four pairs of folded A2 sheets, large-print source book and replaceable folders. Celestial sheets are clearly labelled as Setouchi observer views.

Visitor sequence:

1. Open a labelled drawer or folder.
2. Unfold one world sheet beside its Japan companion.
3. Read a short local text commissioned with the host, then return the sheet for the next visitor.

Planning total: JPY 980,000. These are allowances, not quotations.

Human review: Agree room use, desk access and local text ownership. Test folding durability and give the host spare copies and a clear replacement method.

## Selection and next steps

E1 is the recommended exhibition; V1 is the simplest event; I1 is the simplest independent installation. I2 needs site-specific engineering and should be selected only with a suitable host and realistic fabrication capacity. Review the nine catalogs, choose up to three plans, confirm applicant details and finish the application packet.
